Script coverage, script notes or a script consultant: which do you need?

Writers use these words interchangeably, and the people selling them don't always help. They're different tools built for different jobs. Here's how to pick the one that fits where your script is right now.

The quick difference

Script coverage judges a script for a buyer, script notes tell the writer how to improve it, a script consultant works through those notes with you, and a script doctor rewrites the script themselves. Everything else is a variation on those four.

The confusion is understandable. Services sell "coverage" that's really notes, and "notes" that are really a coverage template. The label matters less than the question underneath it: do you want a verdict, a diagnosis, a collaborator or a rewrite?

Script coverage: a verdict for the buyer

Coverage is a short report that summarizes a script and recommends whether a company should pursue it. It was invented so executives could triage hundreds of submissions without reading them all. I relied on it for years.

That origin tells you its limits. Coverage tells you whether it works for a buyer. It's not designed to tell you how to fix it. Good paid coverage adds notes on top, but the format still leans toward grading. I cover the format in detail in what script coverage is, and whether it's worth paying for in is script coverage worth it?

Script notes: a diagnosis for the writer

Script notes are feedback written for the writer: what isn't working, why, and ideas for fixing it. Notes can be a two-page memo or a marked-up script with comments in the margins.

Good notes do three things. They identify the real problem, which is often not where the symptom shows up. They explain why it matters to an audience. And they offer at least one way forward without rewriting the script for you. Bad notes just list complaints.

The weakness of written notes is that you can't ask follow-up questions. "The protagonist is passive" can mean six different things, and you'll guess at which one the reader meant.

A script consultant: notes plus a conversation

A script consultant reads your script and then works through the problems with you, usually on a call, so you leave with a plan rather than a list. This is what I do.

The conversation is the point. In a live session, you can push back, try out a fix out loud and find out whether a note is a deal-breaker or a nice-to-have. In my experience, the most useful moment in a notes call is usually the writer saying, "Wait, so if I move this scene..." and the whole second act reorganizing itself.

What to look for in a consultant is the same as in any reader: real experience on the development side or as a working writer, a clear process and a price you know up front.

A script doctor: someone else rewrites it

A script doctor is a writer hired, usually by a producer or studio, to rewrite parts of a script that's already in development. They don't advise the writer. They do the work, often fast and often uncredited.

That makes a script doctor the wrong choice for most writers with a spec. If someone else rewrites your script, it stops being a sample of your writing. I go into how the job really works in what does a script doctor do?

Peers, writing groups and mentors

Free feedback from other writers is valuable early, and a mentor is valuable over a whole career, but neither replaces a professional read before a script goes out.

  • Writing groups are great at telling you what's confusing and what's boring. They're less reliable on what a buyer wants, and friends soften notes.
  • Mentors and coaches work with you across drafts and projects. That's a different goal from fixing one script by a deadline.

Which one you need, by situation

Match the feedback to where the script is and what you'll do next.

  • "I just finished a first draft." A writing group or a trusted peer. Save paid feedback for a later draft.
  • "It's close, and I'm sending it to managers next month." Notes or a consultant, so the last rewrite counts.
  • "I keep getting passes and I don't know why." A consultant. You need someone to find the pattern and talk it through.
  • "I want to know how a reader will grade it." Coverage from a reader you can identify.
  • "I'm a producer with a project going into production." That's when you hire a script doctor.
  • "I want to get better over the next few years." A mentor, a class or a program. I teach screenwriting at Chapman University, and there's no shortcut for that kind of growth.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between script coverage and script notes?

Coverage is a short report written for a buyer: a synopsis, comments and a pass, consider or recommend verdict. Script notes are written for the writer and focus on what isn't working and how to fix it.

What does a script consultant do?

A script consultant reads your script and works through the problems with you, usually on a call, so you leave with a plan for the rewrite rather than just a list of issues.

Is a script doctor the same as a script consultant?

No. A script doctor rewrites the script, usually for a producer or studio. A consultant advises the writer, who does the rewriting.

Which kind of feedback should I get before querying agents?

Notes or a consultant read, early enough to rewrite before you send it out. Coverage alone tells you how a reader will grade it, but not how to fix it.
