What a reader looks for in the first 10 pages of a TV pilot

Most pilots are decided before page ten. Not because readers are cruel, but because the opening tells them almost everything about the writer. Here's what they're looking for, page by page.

Why the first ten pages matter so much

The first ten pages of a pilot tell a reader whether the writer can deliver tone, character and a reason to keep watching, and most readers have formed their verdict by then. Everything after that either confirms the verdict or, rarely, overturns it.

When I was reading for a living, the stack never got smaller. Executives, managers and staffing readers are in the same position. Nobody stops at page ten on purpose. But if the opening hasn't earned their attention, the rest is read with one eye, looking for a reason to stop. If it has, they'll forgive a lot later on.

This post is about the opening specifically. For the whole job a pilot has to do, see my guide on how to write a TV pilot.

Page one: tone, world, and something happening

Page one should tell the reader what kind of show this is, where we are, and that something is already happening.

  • Tone first. If it's a comedy, page one should be funny. If it's a thriller, it should be tense. A pilot that opens tonally neutral and "gets going" on page eight has already lost ground.
  • A specific world. Not "a city at night," but a place with rules and texture. The specificity is what makes the world feel like it can hold a series.
  • Action, not setup. A character doing something, ideally something that reveals who they are. Weather and landscape can wait.

Readers also notice the physical page. Dense blocks of description on page one feel like homework. White space signals a writer who knows how scripts are read.

The cold open or teaser: a promise

The cold open is the pilot's first promise to the audience, and in two to five pages it has to make the reader want the next scene.

For a drama, that usually means posing a question: a crime, a secret, a decision we don't understand yet. For a comedy, it means landing a laugh and showing the comic engine of the show in miniature. The best cold opens do both jobs at once: they're entertaining on their own, and they turn out to be about what the series is about.

The most common mistake is a cold open that's just a flash-forward to the most exciting moment, followed by "48 hours earlier." It can work, but readers have seen it so many times that it has to be earned.

By page five: a character worth following

By about page five, the reader should know whose show this is and want to follow that person, even if they don't like them.

"Want to follow" is not the same as "likeable." Plenty of great pilots open on difficult people. What they share is a character who wants something, is doing something about it, and is interesting to watch while they do. A protagonist who only reacts to other characters for the first ten pages reads as passive, and passive protagonists are one of the most common notes in any coverage stack.

Introduce fewer people than you think. Ten named characters in ten pages leaves a reader holding none of them. One or two vivid introductions beat a full ensemble roll call.

By page ten: a hint of the engine

By page ten, the reader should sense the engine of the series, the situation that will keep generating stories week after week.

You don't have to explain the whole series. But a reader should feel the pressure that's going to drive it: the job that brings a new case every week, the lie that has to be maintained, the family business that keeps pulling people back. If page ten could be the opening of a feature film, the reader will start to wonder whether this is really a show.

Voice: why you are the writer of this show

Voice is the thing a pilot sample is really selling, and the first ten pages are where a reader decides whether you have one.

Most original pilots don't get produced. They get read as samples, by reps deciding whether to sign you and showrunners deciding whether to staff you. That reader is asking whether this writer sees the world in a way nobody else on the staff does. Voice shows up in the action lines as much as the dialogue: what you choose to notice, how you describe it, and what you leave out.

What ends the read early

The fastest ways to lose a reader in the opening pages are predictable, and almost all of them are fixable in a rewrite.

  • Exposition dumps. Characters explaining backstory to each other in the first scene.
  • Camera directions. "We PUSH IN on..." in a spec reads as a writer directing on paper.
  • Waking up. An alarm clock, a character getting out of bed. Readers have seen thousands.
  • A slow opening "because it pays off later." It might, but nobody will be there to see it.
  • Tone confusion. A dark drama that stops for a broad joke, or a comedy that opens on a funeral played straight, with no sense of how the two connect.
  • Typos and formatting errors. Fair or not, they tell a reader the writer isn't ready.

A ten-page self-test

Before you send a pilot out, give the first ten pages to someone who knows nothing about the show and ask them five questions.

Ask a cold reader after page ten
  • What kind of show is this?
  • Whose show is it, and what do they want?
  • What do you think happens next week?
  • What line or moment do you remember?
  • Do you want to keep reading? Be honest.

If their answers match what's in your head, the opening is working. If they don't, the gap between the two is your rewrite. And when you get notes back, my guide to taking script notes can help you sort the useful ones from the noise.

Frequently asked questions

Do readers really stop reading a pilot after 10 pages?

Some do, especially with a large stack. More often they keep reading but have already formed a strong opinion. A strong opening buys goodwill for the rest of the script.

How long should the cold open of a TV pilot be?

Usually two to five pages. It should be entertaining on its own and set up the tone and central question of the show.

How many characters should I introduce in the first 10 pages?

As few as the story allows. One or two vivid introductions are better than a long list of names the reader can't keep track of.

Should page one of a pilot start with action?

Yes, in the sense that a character should be doing something that reveals who they are. Long scene-setting description and characters waking up are common openings readers have seen too often.
