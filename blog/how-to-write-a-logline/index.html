How to write a logline: what a studio exec reads first

Before anyone reads your script, they read one sentence about it. After more than 2,000 scripts on my desk, here's what made me open one.

Matt TabakFormer studio executive and WGA writer. Published Sept. 23, 2026

In this post

  1. What a logline is for
  2. The four parts
  3. How long it should be

What a logline is actually for

A logline's job is to make a busy reader want to open your script. It isn't a summary; it's a sales tool. If your logline explains the plot but doesn't create that want, it has failed, no matter how accurate it is.

The four parts of a logline that works

A logline that works has four parts: a specific protagonist, an inciting situation, a goal, and an obstacle with real stakes. Leave any one out and the reader can't picture the movie.

The checklist
  • A specific protagonist, not a generic one
  • A clear inciting event
  • A concrete goal
  • An obstacle with real stakes
  • One sentence, two at most

Notice what's not on the list: theme, backstory, the twist, or the ending. Those matter in the script. In the logline they get in the way.

How long should a logline be?

One sentence, two at most. There's no official word count, but if you can't say it in a single breath, the reader can't hold it in their head either. Length is usually a symptom: a logline runs long when the writer hasn't decided what the movie is about yet.

Frequently asked questions

Should a logline name the characters?

Usually not. A specific description of the protagonist tells a reader more than a name they don't know yet.

Should a logline include the ending?

No. A logline sells the setup and the central conflict. The ending belongs in the synopsis.

What's the difference between a logline and a tagline?

A logline describes the story for industry readers. A tagline is a marketing line for audiences, like the one on a poster.
