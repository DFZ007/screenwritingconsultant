How to pitch or submit a script to Netflix (and what really happens)

There's no form for sending a screenplay to Netflix. Here's how to submit a movie script to Netflix the way projects actually get there, and what to watch out for along the way.

Does Netflix accept unsolicited scripts?

No. If you're wondering how to submit a movie script to Netflix directly, the short answer is that you can't: Netflix does not accept or review unsolicited material. Its Help Center page on how ideas are pitched to Netflix says that pitches must come through a licensed agent, producer, attorney, manager or industry executive who already has a relationship with the company. Netflix also says it won't recommend any of those people to you.

That means there is no inbox, upload form or email address where a writer without representation can send a screenplay and expect it to be read. If you mail or email one anyway, it won't be reviewed. The same Netflix page lists where its projects actually come from: pitches to its creative executives, relationships with the creative community and agencies, finished films acquired at festivals and other venues, and ideas Netflix develops internally and then hires writers to execute.

None of this is unique to Netflix. Every major studio and streamer works the same way. I spent years as an executive at Touchstone/Disney, Paramount and Warner Bros., and on the buying side, unsolicited material doesn't get opened.

Why Netflix and the studios won't read your script cold

The main reason is legal exposure: if a company reads your unsolicited idea and later makes something similar, it can be sued, even if the similarity is a coincidence. Refusing unsolicited material is the simplest way to avoid that risk.

Ideas themselves generally aren't protected by copyright; only the expression of them is. But California courts have recognized claims based on an implied contract to pay for an idea, going back to Desny v. Wilder (1956), a case about a story pitched to Billy Wilder's office. As one entertainment law firm explains, these claims turn on whether the idea was disclosed with an expectation of payment, whether the recipient accepted it on those terms, and whether they used it.

A streamer is developing hundreds of projects at once, and many of them overlap with ideas strangers are mailing in. Every unread script that arrives is a potential lawsuit. So companies refuse them outright, or, when they do agree to look at material from outside, they require a signed submission release that limits what the writer can claim.

Representation solves the problem from their side. When a script arrives through an agent, manager, attorney or producer they know, there's a professional relationship, a paper trail and someone accountable for where the material came from. That's what "solicited" really means. (I'm an attorney licensed in New York, not California, and none of this is legal advice. If you have a question about protecting your work, ask an entertainment attorney.)

How to submit a movie script to Netflix the real way

The realistic path is through someone Netflix already does business with: an agent, a manager, an entertainment attorney or a producer. Your job is to get one of them excited enough to send it.

  • Literary managers are often the first door for newer writers. They tend to sign writers earlier than agents do and help develop material before it goes out. Most managers find new clients through referrals, contest results and well-targeted queries. My post on how to pitch a screenplay covers how to write one.
  • Agents at the larger agencies usually take on writers who already have momentum: a sale, a strong contest result, a manager pushing for them, or a script that's generating heat.
  • Entertainment attorneys can also submit material, and some will "shop" a script for a writer they believe in. They're also who you want reviewing any deal before you sign it.
  • Producers are the route most people overlook, which is why they get their own section below.

Whoever you approach, they're judging the same thing a Netflix executive would: whether the script is ready. Getting a rep is hard. Getting a rep with a script that isn't ready is how you burn the relationship.

Producers with Netflix deals

Many projects reach Netflix through producers who have overall or first-look deals with the company, because those producers bring projects to Netflix as part of the deal. If you can get a script to the right producer, you're one step from a Netflix executive.

A first-look deal gives Netflix the first chance to pass on or pick up a producer's projects. In March 2026, for example, Netflix signed a multiyear first-look streaming deal with Ben Affleck and Matt Damon's Artists Equity. Overall deals, more common in television, pay a writer or producer to make their work exclusively for the company for a period of time.

The trade press reports these deals regularly, so you can find out which companies have them. Then look at what each company makes. A producer with a Netflix first-look who makes grounded thrillers is a better target for your thriller than a bigger name who makes family films. Producers still won't read unsolicited scripts any more than Netflix will, so the approach is the same: a referral or a strong query, sent to a named development executive, asking permission to send.

Fellowships, labs and festivals with Netflix ties

A few writing programs are funded or sponsored by Netflix, and the festival circuit is one of the routes Netflix itself names, but none of them is a submission window for scripts to Netflix. What they offer is exposure, mentorship and introductions to the people who can submit for you.

Programs I can point to as of this writing:

  • Film Independent's Episodic Lab, for writers developing original TV pilots. Its 2026 edition was the lab's tenth, with Netflix returning as founding sponsor. It ends with a pitch and networking event for studio and network executives.
  • The Inevitable Foundation's Accelerate Fellowship, for disabled film and TV writers, which the foundation describes as supported by Netflix. It includes an unrestricted grant and mentorship, and applicants need some existing industry credential, such as representation, a prior sale or a notable competition placement. The 2026 application window has closed.

Eligibility, dates and sponsors change every year, so check each program's own site before you apply. The Writers Guild Foundation keeps a useful master list of fellowships and writing programs.

Festivals are the other route. Netflix acquires finished films at festivals; it bought global rights to Chloe Domont's feature debut Fair Play out of Sundance in 2023. That's a route for a writer who also gets the film made, usually independently, which is a much bigger undertaking than writing a script. But a short film or an indie feature that plays well can introduce you to the agents and executives who matter.

How a project actually gets to a Netflix executive

In practice, it's a chain: your script earns a representative or producer, they bring it to a Netflix executive they know, and that executive has to champion it internally. Each link only happens if the previous one is excited.

A typical path looks like this. You write a script good enough to place in a respected contest or get a manager's attention. The manager sends it around town as a writing sample or as a spec for sale. It gets you general meetings with producers and executives. A producer attaches to a project, either your script or an idea you pitch them, and takes it to a Netflix executive they work with. If that executive likes it, they have to pitch it again to their own bosses before anything is bought.

Very often the first thing a script gets you isn't a sale at all. It's a meeting, an assignment to write something else, or a relationship that pays off years later. That's normal. The track record you build along the way, from placements and produced shorts to staffing jobs and assignments, is what makes the next script easier to send.

How to pitch a script to Netflix once you're in the room

Pitching to Netflix is like pitching anywhere else: a clear logline, a character we care about, the big moments, the ending and the reason it's yours to write. The difference is knowing who you're pitching to.

Netflix is organized into teams by format, genre and region, and each executive is buying for a specific slate. Your representative will usually know what a given executive is looking for. Watch what that team has released recently. Know where your project fits, and why a global audience would want it. Be ready for questions about audience and budget.

And make it easy to repeat. The executive across from you is not the last person who has to say yes, so give them a logline and a couple of moments they can retell upstairs in two minutes.

Beware of "submit to Netflix" services and scams

Be skeptical of any company or person who charges you a fee to get your script to Netflix. Netflix's own policy says it only takes submissions through people it already has relationships with, and it doesn't refer writers to anyone.

The industry watchdog Writer Beware has documented fake literary agencies and "film scouts" that contact writers out of the blue, praise their work, and then ask for money for screenplays, pitch packages or pitch events. Warning signs:

  • They contacted you first, with vague praise.
  • They charge upfront to pitch, represent or "submit" your work. Legitimate agents and managers are paid a commission when you're paid.
  • They promise access to Netflix or another named streamer.
  • They pressure you with deadlines or "once in a lifetime" events.
  • Their credits can't be verified anywhere but their own website.

Paying for notes, coverage or a contest entry is different, as long as you know what you're buying: someone's judgment about your script, not a door into a company. Ask who is actually reading it and what they've done. Then spend your effort on the script itself, because a great script is still the one thing every route above requires.

Frequently asked questions

Can I send my script directly to Netflix?

No. Netflix says it does not accept or review unsolicited material, and that pitches must come through a licensed agent, producer, attorney, manager or industry executive it already works with.

Does Netflix have a screenwriting contest?

Netflix doesn't take scripts through an open submission contest of its own, but it funds or sponsors some writing programs, such as Film Independent's Episodic Lab. These offer mentorship and introductions, not a direct sale to Netflix.

Should I pay a service to submit my script to Netflix?

Be very skeptical. Netflix only takes material from people it already has relationships with and doesn't refer writers to anyone, so a fee-based promise of access is a warning sign.

Do I need an agent to sell a script to Netflix?

You need someone Netflix already works with: an agent, manager, entertainment attorney or producer. For many new writers, a manager or a producer is the first realistic door.

Can you submit my script to Netflix?

No. I give notes and career guidance, not submissions. I can help you get the script ready and think through how to approach representatives and producers.
