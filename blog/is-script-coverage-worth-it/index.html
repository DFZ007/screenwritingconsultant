Is script coverage worth it? An honest answer from the other side of the desk

Coverage can save you a year of sending out a script that isn't ready. It can also be a few hundred dollars for a grade you already suspected. Here's how to tell which one you're buying.

The short answer

Paid script coverage is worth it when your script is finished, you're about to send it to people who matter, and the reader is someone whose opinion you'd actually trust. It's usually not worth it for a first draft, as a way to find out if you're "good," or from a reader you know nothing about.

I spent years on the buying side, reading coverage written by studio readers and acting on it. Coverage is a tool for making a decision quickly. Whether it's worth paying for depends on whether you need a decision or a diagnosis. Most writers need the diagnosis.

If you're not sure what coverage even contains, start with my post on what script coverage is, or look at a full screenplay coverage example.

What you're actually paying for

When you buy coverage, you're paying for one reader's time, judgment and experience, packaged as a short report with a verdict. The report is the cheap part. The judgment is what you're paying for.

At a studio, coverage exists so an executive doesn't have to read every script that comes in. The reader's job is to summarize the story, grade it and say pass, consider or recommend. That format was built for the buyer, not for the writer. When you pay a service for coverage, you're buying a version of that same report, sometimes with extra notes added.

So the questions that decide whether it's worth the money are simple:

Before you pay, ask
  • Who is the reader? A name, a background and real development or writing credits, or an anonymous "industry professional"?
  • What will I get? A grade, a list of problems, or actual ideas for how to fix them?
  • Can I ask questions? A report you can't respond to leaves you guessing what the reader meant.
  • What will I do with it? If you don't plan to rewrite, the money buys you a feeling, not a better script.

The price tiers, and what each one gets you

Coverage ranges from a few dollars for an AI report to several hundred dollars for a detailed read from an experienced professional, and the difference is mostly who is reading and how much they engage.

  • AI coverage (roughly $10 to $40). Instant and cheap. One competitor's own comparison chart lists AI coverage tools in that range with turnaround measured in minutes. It can summarize your plot and flag formatting issues. It can't tell you whether a scene works in a room full of people, or whether a buyer would want it. I don't use AI to read scripts, and I'd treat AI coverage as a spellcheck, not a verdict.
  • Budget human coverage (under about $150). A real reader, often early in their career, using a template. Useful for catching obvious problems: a slow first act, a confusing timeline, a protagonist who disappears. The quality varies a lot from reader to reader.
  • Premium coverage (about $200 and up). More pages of notes and, often, a reader you can choose. For example, Script Reader Pro lists feature coverage from $199. At this level you should expect specific, script-level suggestions, not boilerplate.
  • A consultant or live notes call. Someone with real industry experience reads the script and talks it through with you. This is less "grade my script" and more "help me fix it." My own verbal coverage works this way, and so do my screenplay notes.

Price alone doesn't tell you much. A $60 read from a sharp reader can beat a $400 report from someone coasting. What matters is the person.

When paid coverage is worth it

Coverage earns its price at specific moments in a script's life, usually right before it goes out into the world.

  • Before you query managers or agents. You get one shot with most reps. A professional read beforehand is cheap insurance.
  • Before a contest or fellowship deadline. Especially the big ones, where the first-round readers work much like studio readers do.
  • When friends have run out of useful things to say. Your writing group has read it three times. Fresh eyes with no stake in your feelings will see things they can't.
  • When you keep getting passes and don't know why. A pattern of polite rejections usually means the same problem is showing up early. A good reader can find it.
  • When you're stuck on a rewrite. You know something is wrong and can't see what. That's exactly what an experienced reader is for.

When it isn't worth it

Coverage is a poor use of money when the script isn't ready to be judged, or when you're buying reassurance instead of information.

  • On a first draft. You already know most of what's wrong with it. Do another pass yourself first.
  • To find out if you're "talented." One reader's grade can't answer that, and a "pass" on draft one tells you nothing about draft five.
  • From several services at once. Five reports on the same draft mostly produce five sets of contradictory opinions. One great read beats several mediocre ones.
  • When you've decided not to change anything. If you're sending it out regardless, save the money for postage, metaphorically speaking.

Red flags in a coverage service

The biggest red flag is a reader you can't identify; the second biggest is a promise of anything other than an honest read. Watch for:

  • Anonymous readers. "Our readers have worked at major studios" with no names attached could mean anything.
  • Guarantees of representation or a sale. No reader can promise that. Anyone who does is selling the dream, not the read.
  • Boilerplate notes. If a sample report could apply to any script ("the stakes could be higher," "the second act sags"), the read you get probably will too.
  • No sample. A legitimate service should show you what you're buying.
  • Hidden AI. If the service is fast and cheap, ask directly whether a person reads every page.

How to get your money's worth

Send your best draft, tell the reader what you're trying to do, and plan the rewrite before the notes arrive.

  • Send the draft you'd send to an agent. Not the one you're "pretty sure" about. You're paying for notes on what you can't see, not what you could have fixed yourself.
  • Include a logline and your goal. "I'm sending this to managers next month" gets a different, more useful read than no context at all. If your logline is shaky, my guide on how to write a logline helps.
  • Read the notes twice, a day apart. The first read is emotional. The second is useful.
  • Look for the note behind the note. When a reader says "the ending doesn't work," the problem is often 60 pages earlier. My post on how to take script notes goes into this.

Coverage is worth it when it changes the next draft. If it doesn't, it was just an expensive opinion.

Frequently asked questions

How much does script coverage cost?

It ranges widely. AI coverage tools can cost $10 to $40, budget human coverage is usually under about $150, and premium coverage from experienced readers often starts around $200. A live notes call with a consultant costs more but includes a conversation about how to fix the script.

Is AI script coverage any good?

It can summarize a plot and catch formatting problems quickly. It can't judge whether a scene lands, whether a character is compelling or whether a buyer would want the project. Treat it as a spellcheck, not a verdict.

Should I get coverage on my first draft?

Usually not. You already know most of what's wrong with a first draft. Do at least one more pass yourself, then pay for an outside read when the script is close to going out.

Can coverage help me get an agent or manager?

Indirectly. Coverage can't get you represented, and anyone who promises that is overselling. But a good read before you query can catch the problems that would otherwise get you a pass.
